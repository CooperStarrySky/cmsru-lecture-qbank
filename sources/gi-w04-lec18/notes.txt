Cystic fibrosis (CF)
Cystic fibrosis (abbreviated as CF) is a genetic disorder that affects the lungs, pancreas, liver, and intestine.
CF is caused by a mutation in the gene encoding the protein Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane conductance
Regulator (CFTR). This protein is involved in the regulation of the components of sweat, digestive fluids,
and mucus. CFTR regulates the movement of chloride and sodium ions across epithelial membranes, such as
those in the lungs. High levels of salt in the sweat of patients with cystic fibrosis suggested an abnormality in
fluid and electrolyte transport in the sweat gland, and it was established that sweat ducts in such patients are
impermeable to chloride. In the airway, cystic fibrosis patients have decreased Cl- secretion and increased
Na+ and water re-absorption leading to dehydration of the mucus layer, which finally leads to mucus
plugging of airways. The abnormally viscous secretions that obstruct organ passages result in most of the
clinical features of this disorder, such as chronic lung disease secondary to recurrent infections, pancreatic
insufficiency, steatorrhea, malnutrition, hepatic cirrhosis, intestinal obstruction, and male infertility. These
manifestations may appear at any point in life from before birth to much later in childhood or even in
adolescence.
Pancreatic disease: During normal digestion stomach acid is neutralized by pancreatic bicarbonate, leading
to the optimal pH for pancreatic enzyme action. Reduced bicarbonate secretion has been demonstrated in CF
patients regardless of pancreatic function. Reduced bicarbonate secretion inhibits proper digestion because
pancreatic enzymes are optimally active at basic pH and mutated CFTR affects bicarbonate transport in the
gut directly. In addition, reduced water content of secretions, precipitation of proteins, and plugging of
ductules and acini, prevent the pancreatic enzymes from reaching the gut. Packaging proteases for secretion
is potentially dangerous for a cell and many proteases are made in an inactive form, only to be activated
when secreted to the right environment. In CF, pancreatic proteases begin to accumulate in the organ because
of blockages. Eventually the proteases are released inside the pancreatic cells and become activated leading
to autodigestion of the pancreas and pancreatitis. Most patients with cystic fibrosis (90-95%) have pancreatic
enzyme insufficiency and present with digestive symptoms and/or failure to thrive early in life. Onset of
pancreatic insufficiency varies, however, and may occur in patients older than 6 months.
Patients with pancreatic insufficiency typically present with poor weight gain in association with frequent
stools that are bulky, malodorous, greasy, and associated with flatulence and colicky pain after
feeding. The combination of increased energy intake demand at baseline, the added energy intake demand of
chronic disease, difficulty sustaining energy uptake because of malabsorption, and anorexia associated with
ongoing lung inflammation leads to poor weight gain. Pancreatic insufficiency predisposes patients to poor
absorption of fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K. Symptomatic deficiency of any of these vitamins can
occur before diagnosis or as a later complication of the disease.
Liver disease: Absence of functional CFTR in epithelial cells lining the biliary ductules leads to reduced
secretion of chloride and reduction in passive transport of water and chloride, resulting in increased viscosity
of bile. The biliary ductules may be plugged with secretions. If this process is extensive, obstructive cirrhosis
complicated by esophageal varices, splenomegaly, and hypersplenism may occur.

Gallbladder: Gallstones are more prevalent in patients with cystic fibrosis and as many as 15% of young
adults with cystic fibrosis have gallstones, irrespective of the status of their pancreatic function. Abnormal
mucin in the gallbladder and malabsorption of bile acids in patients with CF result in a higher frequency of
gallstones.
Celiac disease (CD)
Celiac disease is an autoimmune disorder, previously called celiac sprue. Celiac disease is precipitated, in
genetically predisposed persons, by the ingestion of gluten, the major storage protein of wheat and similar
grains. Originally considered a rare malabsorption syndrome of childhood, celiac disease is now recognized
as a common condition that may be diagnosed at any age and that affects many organ systems.
Pathogenesis
Celiac disease results from the interaction between gluten and immune, genetic, and environmental factors
The role of gluten
Celiac disease is induced by the ingestion of gluten, which is derived from wheat, barley, and rye. The gluten
protein is enriched in glutamine and proline and is poorly digested in the human upper gastrointestinal tract.
The term “gluten” refers to the entire protein component of wheat; gliadin is the alcohol-soluble fraction of
gluten that contains the bulk of the toxic components. Undigested molecules of gliadin are resistant to
degradation by gastric, pancreatic, and intestinal brush-border membrane proteases in the human intestine
and thus remain in the intestinal lumen after gluten ingestion. These peptides pass through the epithelial
barrier of the intestine, possibly during intestinal infections or when there is an increase in intestinal
permeability, and interact with antigen-presenting cells in the lamina propria.
Mucosal immune responses
In patients with celiac disease, immune responses to gliadin fractions promote an inflammatory reaction,
primarily in the upper small intestine, characterized by infiltration of the lamina propria and the epithelium
with chronic inflammatory cells and villous atrophy. This response is mediated by both the innate and the
adaptive immune systems. The adaptive response is mediated by gliadin-reactive CD4+ T cells in the lamina
propria that recognize gliadin peptides, which are bound to HLA class II molecules DQ2 or DQ8 on antigenpresenting cells; the T cells subsequently produce proinflammatory cytokines, particularly interferonγ. Tissue transglutaminase is an enzyme in the intestine that deamidates gliadin peptides, increasing their
immunogenicity. The ensuing inflammatory cascade releases metalloproteinases and other tissue-damaging
mediators that induce crypt hyperplasia and villous injury. Gliadin peptides also activate an innate immune
response in the intestinal epithelium that is characterized by increased expression of interleukin-15 by
enterocytes, resulting in the activation of intraepithelial lymphocytes expressing the activating receptor NKG2D, a natural-killer-cell marker. These activated cells become cytotoxic and kill enterocytes with surface
expression of major-histocompatibility-complex class I chain-related A (MIC-A), a cell-surface antigen
induced by stress, such as an infection. The mechanism of the interaction between the processes in the
epithelium and lamina propria has not been elucidated.
Genetic Factors
Celiac disease does not develop unless a person has alleles that encode for HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8 proteins,
products of two of the HLA genes. However, many people, most of whom do not have celiac disease, carry
these alleles; thus, their presence is necessary but not sufficient for the development of the disease.
Environmental Factors
Environmental factors include a protective effect of breast-feeding and the introduction of gluten in relation
to weaning. The initial administration of gluten before 4 months of age is associated with an increased risk of

However, the overlap of gluten introduction with breast-feeding may be a more important protective factor in

Epidemiology
Celiac disease occurs in adults and children at rates approaching 1% of the population. The disease is
recognized not only throughout Europe and in countries populated by persons of European ancestry but also
in the Middle East, Asia, South America and North Africa. In most affected people, celiac disease remains
undiagnosed, although the rate of diagnosis is increasing.
Clinical manifestations
Clinical manifestations of celiac disease vary greatly according to age group. Infants and young children
generally present with diarrhea, abdominal distention, and failure to thrive. However, vomiting, irritability,
anorexia, and even constipation are also common. Older children and adolescents often present with
extraintestinal manifestations, such as short stature, neurologic symptoms, or anemia. Among adults, two
to three times as many women have the disease as men, for unknown reasons. The classic presentation in
adults is diarrhea, which may be accompanied by abdominal pain or discomfort. Other presentations in adults
include iron-deficiency anemia, osteoporosis, and incidental recognition at endoscopy performed for other
reasons, such as symptoms of gastroesophageal reflux. Abdominal pain, constipation, weight loss, neurologic
symptoms, dermatitis herpetiformis, hypoproteinemia, hypocalcemia, and elevated liver enzyme levels are
also seen. Some cases are diagnosed because of increased surveillance for celiac disease among people who
have a family history of the disease and among those with Down syndrome, Turner's syndrome, or type
1diabetes, all of which are associated with celiac disease. Persons with celiac disease have an increased risk
of autoimmune disorders as compared with the general population.
Diagnosis
The diagnosis of celiac disease requires both a duodenal biopsy that shows the characteristic findings of
intraepithelial lymphocytosis, crypt hyperplasia, and villous atrophy and a positive response to a gluten-free
diet. Villous atrophy may persist despite a clinical response to the diet. In most patients, the diagnosis is
easily established. However, roughly 10% of cases are difficult to diagnose because of a lack of concordance
among serologic, clinical, and histologic findings.
Serological testing
Typical indications for serologic testing include unexplained bloating or abdominal distress; chronic
diarrhea, with or without malabsorption or the irritable bowel syndrome; abnormalities on laboratory tests
that might be caused by malabsorption (e.g., folate deficiency and iron-deficiency anemia); first-degree
relatives with CD; and autoimmune diseases and other conditions known to be associated with CD.
The most sensitive antibody tests for the diagnosis of CD are of the IgA class. The most used test is for
antibodies directed against tissue transglutaminase, the enzyme responsible for the deamidation of gliadin
in the lamina propria. The endomysial IgA antibodies are highly specific markers for celiac disease,
approaching 100% accuracy. Overall, the sensitivity of the tests for both endomysial antibodies and anti–
tissue transglutaminase antibodies is greater than 90%, and a test for either marker is considered the best
means of screening for CD.
HLA-DQ2 and HLA-DQ8
HLA-DQ2 allele is identified in 90 to 95% of patients with celiac disease, and HLA-DQ8 is identified in most
of the remaining patients. Because these alleles occur in 30 to 40% of the general population (with HLA3

DQ2 more common than HLA-DQ8), the absence of these alleles is important for its high negative predictive
value. Thus, the presence or absence of HLA-DQ2 andHLA-DQ8 is important for determining which family
members should be screened with serologic testing and is useful for ruling out the disease in patients already
on a gluten-free diet or for patients in whom the diagnosis is unclear.
Histological assessment
Biopsy of the small intestine remains the standard for diagnosing celiac disease. The spectrum of pathologic
changes in celiac disease ranges from near-normal villous architecture with a prominent intraepithelial
lymphocytosis to total villous atrophy.
Nutritional therapy
Nutritional therapy involves the lifelong elimination of wheat, rye, and barley from the diet. Meats, dairy
products, and fruits and vegetables are naturally gluten-free and help to make for a more nutritious and
varied diet. After the diagnosis of celiac disease has been established, the patient should be assessed for
deficiencies of vitamins and minerals, including folic acid, B12, fat-soluble vitamins, iron, and calcium, and
any such deficiencies should be treated. All patients with celiac disease should undergo screening for
osteoporosis, which has a high prevalence in this population. The health care team should include a skilled
dietitian who monitors the patient's nutritional status and dietary adherence on a regular basis. In children,
ongoing evaluation includes monitoring of growth and development.
Complications of celiac disease
Although the majority of patients who have recurrent or new symptoms when on a supposedly gluten-free
diet are in fact ingesting gluten, either intentionally or unintentionally, a substantial proportion may have a
serious complication of celiac disease: intestinal adenocarcinoma, enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma,
or refractory sprue (persistent symptoms and villous atrophy despite scrupulous adherence to a gluten-free
diet; diarrhea, weight loss, recurrence of malabsorption, abdominal pain, bleeding, and anemia).
Short bowel syndrome
In adults Crohn’s disease, malignancy, radiation and acute vascular insult with bowel necrosis may require
massive resection of the small intestine. Removal of such large portion of the small bowel leads to a
malabsorptive state called as the short bowel syndrome. In children necrotizing enterocolitis is the most
common cause for major bowel resection. These surgical resections are usually the result of some emergent
catastrophic event for the patient. Jejunal resection leads to temporary loss of absorption. However the ileum
will adapt to this loss over time and help to compensate for the malabsorptive process. However, with a
major portion of the ileum resected, the osmotic load from above may overwhelm its absorptive capacity.
The ileum will adapt with growth both in length and diameter as well as lengthening of the villae themselves.
The colon will also increase its absorptive capacity as well in order to accommodate to the situation.
Resection of 60 cm of ileum results in vitamin B-12 deficiency because of malabsorption. Loss of 100 cm or
more of ileum results in bile salt malabsorption and bile salt diarrhea. Loss of the terminal ileum is also a
loss of regulation and control of the passage of contents into the colon. Without the terminal ileum the
enterohepatic recycling of bile salts is lost. The liver acts to replace these bile salts but a net deficiency of
bile salts will result over time nonetheless. In addition, deconjugation of these bile salts by gut bacteria
causes a return of these salts to the original bile acid state. These bile acids are ineffective in fat absorption
(micellization) and therefore steatorrhea results. Moreover, the non-absorbed bile salts cause a secretory
diarrhea in the colon. Finally, gastrin levels increase in many patients with short bowel syndrome. The loss
of the negative feedback mechanism and increased acid production of gastrin disrupts digestion not unlike
that seen in Zollinger Ellison syndrome.

Small Intestinal Bacterial Overgrowth (SIBO)
The consequence of SIBO is nutrient malabsorption. Symptoms may not be apparent or may include
bloating, abdominal discomfort, diarrhea, weight loss and dyspepsia thereby simulating symptoms of
functional bowel disease. In the healthy neonate, normal enteric bacteria colonize the GI tract shortly after
birth. The composition of this flora remains constant throughout life. The GI tract has 10 times more
organisms than there are cells in the human body. As many as 500 different organisms can colonize with diet
influencing this. The stomach and small bowel has few organisms (1000/ml). In the terminal ileum the
aerobic organisms are replaced by enterobacter and coliforms in a concentration approaching 100 million/ml.
Enteric bacteria help physiologic function by a) further digesting dietary sugars so they may be absorbed b)
produce vitamin K and folate c) protect the host from pathological bacteria d) metabolize drugs so as they
can be pharmacologically active (e.g. sulfasalizine is metabolized to 5‐aminsalicylic acid in treating IBD).
Gastric acid and bile destroy bacteria in the stomach, while digestive enzymes destroy bacteria in the small
intestine. The ileocecal valve blocks retrograde migration of bacteria into the small intestine. Secretory IGA
is the largest fraction of immunoglobulins secreted in the body to prevent bacterial proliferation. Gut flora is
influenced by several factors such as diet, medication, stress, lifestyle, geography and alcohol. A number of
seemingly diverse conditions lead to SIBO. The majority are caused by intestinal dysmotility. A complex
and often intertwined series of events lead to malabsorption and diarrhea.
(i) In type I diabetics with autonomic neuropathy, intestinal motility is slowed resulting in stasis. The
inflammatory response in the small bowel may lead to mucosal damage with development of mild lactose
intolerance. Bacteria can also deconjugate bile salts and metabolize carbohydrates into free fatty acids. Both
the bile acids and the free fatty acids promote diarrhea by stimulation of water and electrolyte secretion
especially in the colon. The result of this fatty acid production creates an excess CO2, hydrogen, and
methane causing abdominal distension and flatulence. Fat malabsorption results in steatorrhea. Vitamin B12
absorption is disrupted.
(ii) Fistula from the colon into the small bowel due to Crohn’s disease or colonic malignancy creates a
pathway for bacterial entry from the colon into the small bowel and SIBO.
(iii) Immunodeficiency syndromes, chronic pancreatitis and cystic fibrosis create an environment in the
small bowel conducive to the development of SIBO.
(iv) Scleroderma and its associated formation of multiple wide mouthed diverticulae as a reservoir for
bacterial overgrowth and concomitant dysmotility, become a source of SIBO.
(v) Numerous studies demonstrating the periodic use of antibiotics in various clinical scenarios described
above often improve or temporarily eradicate symptoms of distension, pain and diarrhea.
Fat maldigestion and malabsorption may occur because of deconjugation of bile acids by intraluminal
bacteria, allowing their absorption by the jejunum and leading to insufficient concentrations for micelle
formation and fat absorption. Bacterial deconjugation may also result in the production of substances, such
as lithocholic acid, which may exert toxic effects on the intestinal epithelium and result in impaired
absorption of not only fat, but also carbohydrate and protein. Because of the fat maldigestion and
malabsorption that occurs in the setting of SIBO, deficiencies of the fat‐soluble vitamins A, D and E can
occur. Vitamin K deficiency is rarely seen in SIBO. Carbohydrate malabsorption may also result from the
intraluminal degradation of sugars by enteric bacteria and from bacteria‐related decreases in enterocyte
disaccharidase and brush‐border hydrolase activity, and impaired monosaccharide absorption. Although

Kwashiorkor & Marasmus
Protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) is not a phenomenon limited to developing countries. It can be seen in
hospitalized patients, in individuals with chronic illness and in individuals recovering from surgery or severe
trauma, individuals living in poverty and/or isolation and those with substance abuse problems or have
anorexia nervosa. Sadly, albeit very rarely, it also can be seen in the abused children of neglectful parents,
here in the state of New Jersey. It is also not a rare phenomenon, with WHO estimates of perhaps 5 million
deaths of children under 5 years of age per year attributable to PEM worldwide. The severest forms of these
deficiencies are kwashiorkor and marasmus.
Kwashiorkor is a disease of chronic dietary protein insufficiency, though there is also evidence that
diseases progression is more complicated than that (some hypothesize the presence of key micronutrient
deficiencies to be of at least equal importance). It is most often noted in children, after they are weaned from
breast milk (a very good source of amino acids) onto a carbohydrate rich diet that is poor in essential amino
acids (and in some micronutrients, to be sure). Such a diet may provide adequate calories, but it’s in a
context of poor amino acid nutrition. At the biochemical level this leads to inadequate rates of protein
synthesis, with the net effect being reduced protein inventories in many tissues, with commensurate loss of
tissue function and/or stability. Presentation includes a swollen belly (due to loss of the ability to maintain
water compartments), thinning hair, and dermatitis. Impaired growth and development, and weak immune
responses, accompany this condition. Permanent failure to meet growth milestones is also possible, despite
intervention.
Marasmus is caused by a combined deficiency for both calories and for protein. As such, its presentation
differs somewhat from that of kwashiorkor. As with kwashiorkor, body weight and height are significantly
below growth expectations. However, the caloric deficiency in marasmus has a different consequence for
amino acid and protein metabolism than is seen in kwashiorkor. With severe caloric deficiency, muscle (and
fat) mass is lost due to catabolic turnover of protein (and TAGs) for energy, while there is less dramatic
interruption of visceral organ function. In that regard it is a milder disease (organ and tissue function is
calorie limited, to be sure, which ironically may moderate the adverse effects of dietary amino acid
deficiency).
Vitamins
Vitamins are organic compounds required by the body in small amounts, far below the daily intake of the
macronutrients. Vitamins are needed to maintain normal growth and health. They are necessary for specific
metabolic functions but cannot be synthesized by humans, or cannot be synthesized in sufficient amounts to
meet their needs, and therefore must be supplied in the diet. Vitamins are not energy sources and are not
used for structural purposes. The 13 vitamins are divided into two categories. There are 4 fat soluble
vitamins (vitamins A,D,E,K) and 9 water soluble vitamins consisting of Ascorbic acid (vitamin C) and
the 8 B-complex vitamins (thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, biotin, pantothenic acid, folic acid, vitamin B1

Fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed in the ileum, along with dietary fats, and are stored in the liver and adipose
for potentially long periods of time. Hence, efficient uptake of diet-sourced fat-soluble vitamins requires

Vitamin A
Daily adult requirement of vitamin A can be obtained in the form of retinyl esters from animal sources (egg
yolks, liver, fish oil, milk, butter), which provide the largest available amounts, or in the form of -carotene
(green and orange vegetables).
 Retinol – transport form
 Retinyl esters -storage form
 Retinal – form involved in vision
 Retinoic acid – active in epithelial differentiation and growth
 -carotene (dietary, in vegetables)-cleaved and converted to retinol in intestinal mucosa
Why is vitamin A essential?
 Vision in reduced light: phototransduction, in association with rod and cone cell visual pigments. The
visual pigment of the rod cells is rhodopsin, which consists of the protein opsin bound to the prosthetic
group (chromophore) II-cis retinal. Exposure to light leads to bleaching of the visual pigment and release
of opsin and all trans retinal, together with generation of a nerve impulse. Rhodopsin is the most light
sensitive pigment and therefore the most important in reduced light.
 Hormones that regulate gene expression (retinol, retinoic acid). Involved in differentiation of mucussecreting epithelium.
 Enhanced immunity, resistance to infections
 Glycoprotein and mucopolysacharide synthesis (retinyl phosphate)
 Anti-oxidant (-carotene)
 Bone remodeling
Vitamin A deficiency
Vitamin A is the most widespread worldwide vitamin deficiency. It is common in developing countries and
the leading cause of blindness in world.
 Vision problems
 Night blindness
 Xerophthalmia (dry eye)
 Defects in the maintenance of healthy epithelial tissues (acne and psoriasis)
 Increased susceptibility to infection, leading to higher mortality rates from common diseases such as
measles, infectious diarrhea.
Vitamin A Toxicity
Vitamin A is stored in the liver. High doses of vitamin A over a period of months or years can lead to bone
pain, dermatitis, liver and spleen enlargement, nausea and diarrhea, and birth defects. Because of its storage
and accumulation, vitamin A toxicity is a real concern. The -carotenes, however, show no toxicities, though
elevated consumption can lead to a yellowing of the skin.

Rickets (in children) is the improper mineralization of cartilage and osteoid matrix, leading to soft bones.
Because bones are growing in children, the clinical picture is complex, ultimately including the possibility of
permanent deformation of the skeleton. Lordosis of the spine and/or bowing of the legs are notable clinical
presentations.
Osteomalacia (in adults) is the demineralization of existing bone causing weakening and tendency to
fracture, without gross changes in bone contour.

Osteoporosis
Skeletal growth (modeling) continues into the third decade of life. After that time, bone maintenance
exclusively takes the form of remodeling, a balance of activity by osteoclasts and osteoblasts.
Vitamin D toxicity: Extremely excessive levels of vitamin D (100,000IU for weeks or months) are toxic and
can lead to loss of appetite, nausea, thirst and stupor. Very high levels of vitamin D (above 10,000 IUs per
day) are known to cause kidney and tissue damage.

Role of Vitamin K
The principal action of vitamin K is in blood clotting. Vitamin K is the only fat-soluble vitamin that acts as a
coenzyme. It acts as a coenzyme for the carboxylase that converts glutamic acid residues to carboxyglutamic acid residues, in prothrombin and clotting factors VII, IX and X. The clotting factors are all
synthesized as inactive precursor molecules, and the carboxylation of these specific glutamic acid residues is
necessary before they can be activated. The carboxylated glutamic acid residues are capable of binding
calcium, and the calcium-prothrombin complex can bind to phospholipids, which is essential for clotting on
the platelet surface. Proteins other than those in the clotting cascade have been found to undergo vitamin K
mediated carboxylation of glutamic acid residues, e.g. osteocalcin in bone. It has been suggested that vitamin
K may favor calcification of bone.
Vitamin K Deficiency
Deficiency leads to hypoprothrombinemia and increased coagulation times.
 Vitamin K is synthesized by bacteria in the gut so deficiency is unusual except in newborns.
Hemorrhagic bleeding can occur. Vitamin K stores in neonates may be low because of poor transference
across the placenta, and neonates have sterile intestines. In addition, human milk and cow milk do not
provide adequate vitamin K. Thus, it is recommended that all newborns receive a single injection of
vitamin K as a precaution again bleeding.
 Broad spectrum antibiotic treatments will also decrease the bacterial population in the gut and could lead
to vitamin K deficiency in vulnerable populations.
 Problems with fat absorption (e.g. Cystic fibrosis) may lead to vitamin K deficiency.
Vitamin K Toxicity
The storage of vitamin K in liver is not so high, so its toxicity is rare. Natural forms are not toxic, but large
amounts of foods with high vitamin K content could interfere with Coumadin action. Infants receiving
prolonged administration of large amounts of synthetic vitamin K (K3) can develop hemolytic anemia and
jaundice, probably due to toxic effects on red blood cells.
Anticoagulants: Warfarin (brand name Coumadin) is a vitamin K antagonist used to inhibit clotting, by
preventing vitamin K from getting reduced back into its active form. At least 2 million people are taking
warfarin in the US, to prevent blot clots (atrial fibrillation, artificial heart values, deep venous thrombosis,
pulmonary embolism). But use of warfarin carries risk of bleeding and hemorrhage, and is not compatible
with having surgery. A person on warfarin must be monitored for clotting time, and may show bruising or
other bleeding signs.

Vitamin E
Vitamin E is a family of tocopherol compounds, with -tocopherol the most common and active. Vegetable
oils are rich sources of vitamin E; liver and eggs contain moderate amounts. Vitamin E is the least toxic of
the fat-soluble vitamins; no toxicity has been observed at doses of 300 mg/day.
Role of Vitamin E
Vitamin E is absorbed into the lymphatic system by the small intestine. Its biologically active form tocopherol is transported into the blood after incorporation into very low density lipoproteins (VLDL) in the
liver, a process that requires -tocopherol transport protein. Vitamin E is the most effective lipid soluble
antioxidant, protecting membranes from free radical and lipid peroxidation induced damage.
Deficiency of vitamin E
Vitamin E deficiency is largely restricted to premature infants. When observed in children or adults, it is
usually associated either with defective lipid absorption due to cystic fibrosis, abetalipoproteinaemia, or
chronic cholestasis (where bile cannot flow from the liver to the duodenum); or due to a transport defect
such as mutation in the -tocopherol transport protein. Vitamin E deficiency causes a distinct neurological
disorder including spinocerebellar degeneration and mild to moderate axonal sensory neuropathy, together
with ataxia, areflexia, and other symptoms. In adults, symptoms may develop after ~10-20 years of
malabsorption.

Water soluble vitamins
Water soluble vitamins are excreted in the urine, and there are generally not significant stores in the body.
They usually function as cofactors of enzymes. Some vitamins, e.g. vitamin B12, are coenzymes for only a
small number of enzymes, while other vitamins, e.g. niacin in NADH, are involved in hundreds of reactions.
Coenzymes are small organic molecules bound to enzymes and required for activity. Most coenzymes are
non-covalently bound, and are regenerated at the end of the reaction, so very small amounts are required.

Thiamine (Vitamin B-1)

Thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) is the biologically active form of the vitamin, formed by the transfer of a
pyrophosphate group from ATP to thiamine. All animals require a dietary supply of thiamine, which is
synthesized by plants or microorganisms. TPP is a cofactor used by many enzymes. It serves as a cofactor in
the oxidative decarboxylation of -keto acids, e.g. pyruvate dehydrogenase and -ketoglutarate
dehydrogenase reactions. It is also required for transketolase in the pentose-phosphate pathway, and appears
to be required for synthesis of acetylcholine and therefore nerve signal transmission.
Thiamine Deficiency
The oxidation of pyruvate and -ketoglutarate play a key role in the energy metabolism of most tissues. If
there is thiamine deficiency, the activity of these two dehydrogenase reactions is decreased, resulting in a
decreased production of ATP and impaired cellular function. Thiamine deficiency will lead to a buildup of
pyruvate levels, along with reduced levels of ATP, ribose (precursor to nucleic acids), and NADPH (as a cofactor for fatty acid synthesis).
Well documented syndromes result from thiamine deficiency. Beri-beri is a severe thiamine deficiency
syndrome found in areas where polished rice is the major component of the diet.

Wet beriberi impacts cardiovascular function and leads to swelling of the legs, fluid in the lungs and
rapid heart rate.
Dry beriberi affects the nerves and muscles, causing memory loss and mental confusion, pain in the
hands and feet, involuntary eye movements and trouble standing and moving.
Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome is most commonly seen in chronic alcoholics, and may be due to
dietary insufficiency as a result of impaired intestinal absorption of the vitamin. This state is
characterized by mental confusion, loss of eye coordination, and ataxia.
Riboflavin (Vitamin B-2)

Riboflavin is synthesized by all green plants, and by most bacteria and fungi, but not by animals. Riboflavin
is used in the synthesis of FMN (flavin mononucleotide) and FAD (flavin dinucleotide). Riboflavin kinase
synthesizes FMN from riboflavin and ATP. FAD is synthesized by subsequent adenylation of FMN by FAD
synthetase. The flavin containing cofactors, FMN or FAD, are utilized by about 150 enzymes, including
NADH dehydrogenase (complex I of the electron transport chain), succinate dehydrogenase (complex II of
the electron transport chain), and methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase.
Riboflavin deficiency is not associated with a major human disease, but it frequently accompanies other
vitamin deficiencies and can lead to skin inflammation and breakdown around the mouth (cheilosis), nose,
tongue.

Niacin (Vitamin B-3)
Synthesis of niacin occurs in almost all organisms, from plants to humans. In humans, tryptophan can be
converted to niacin, but this does not supply sufficient niacin to meet the needs of the body. Niacin is thus
classified as a vitamin. Niacin or nicotinic acid, is amidated to form nicotinamide, which is the building
block for both NAD and NADP. Many different enzymes use NAD or NADP as coenzymes in oxidation
reduction reactions, such as occur in the synthesis of fatty acids, cholesterol and steroid hormones.
Niacin Deficiency
Deficiency of niacin leads to pellagra, a disease involving the skin, GI tract, and CNS. The symptoms of
pellagra progress through Dermatitis, Diarrhea, Dementia and if untreated, Death. (Remember the 4 “D”s).
Pyridoxin (Vitamin B-6)
Pyridoxine is synthesized in green plants and many microorganisms. There are three forms of vitamin B6, all
derivatives of pyridine, differing only in the nature of the functional group attached to the ring: PyridoxalPhosphate (PLP) is the active cofactor form, serving as a coenzyme for 112 enzymes involved in the
synthesis, interconversion, and catabolism of amino acids and heme. It functions in energy production from
amino acids and is also involved in the synthesis of serotonin and norepinephrine, and the sphingolipids
involved in myelinization. The cofactor forms a covalent linkage (Schiff base) with a lysyl residue in the
enzyme which gets converted to substrate-PLP when PLP is attacked by a substrate amino group.
Pyridoxin Deficiency
Deficiencies in PLP can result in multiple clinical problems because of its involvement in so many enzyme
reactions. Behavioral changes may be noted because of deficiencies in neurotransmitters and myelin
synthesis, while anemia can also result. Isoniazid is a common drug used for the treatment of tuberculosis.
Vitamin B6 binds very tightly to isoniazid, and becomes unavailable to act as a co-enzyme.
Biotin (Vitamin B-7)
Biotin is made by bacteria, and under ordinary circumstances, sufficient quantities of biotin are provided to
mammals by intestinal bacterial synthesis. Biotin is a coenzyme in carboxylation reactions, serving as a
carrier of activated carbon dioxide. Biotin is covalently bound to the -amino groups of lysine residues of
biotin-dependent enzymes. Biotin is a coenzyme of pyruvate carboxylase, which catalyzes the carboxylation
of pyruvate to form oxaloacetate, the first step in gluconeogenesis. Carbon dioxide is activated by the energy
from ATP to bind to the biotin while it is attached to the lysine on the enzyme, and then the carbon dioxide is
transferred to pyruvate to produce oxaloacetate. Biotin is a coenzyme in a significant number of other
carboxylation reactions as well, e.g. cytosolic acetyl-CoA carboxylase, mitochondrial propionyl-CoA
carboxylase, etc.
Biotin Deficiency
Biotin is found in numerous foods and also is synthesized by intestinal bacteria and deficiencies of the
vitamin are rare. Deficiencies are generally seen only after long antibiotic therapies which deplete the
intestinal fauna or following excessive consumption of raw eggs. The latter is due to the affinity of the egg
white protein, avidin, for biotin preventing intestinal absorption of the biotin.

Cobalamin (Vitamin B-12)
Absorption of vitamin B-12: The absorption of vitamin B-12 is a complex process. Two key proteins to
remember in this process are the R protein (a cobalamin-binding protein present in the saliva) and the
Intrinsic Factor (IF, a protein secreted by the parietal cells of the stomach, which promotes the absorption
of vitamin B-12 in the ileum, via a specific receptor). Vitamin B-12 is bound to proteins in the food. In the
stomach, the acid and pepsin release vitamin B-12 from these proteins, so that the free vitamin B-12 can bind
to the R protein. In the small intestine, trypsin from pancreas releases R protein from vitamin B-12, which
then can bind to the intrinsic factor. The vitamin B-12 and intrinsic factor complex is absorbed into blood
and binds to the transport protein transcobalamine II. The vitamin B-12-transcobalamine II complex then
enters the portal vein and is delivered to liver. As mentioned above, vitamin B-12 can be stored in the liver
for years. (For a quick review of the steps involved in the absorption of Vitamin B-12, see the animation
presented during the lecture).
Defects in the absorption of vitamin B-12: The absorption of vitamin B-12 may be affected due to various
reasons such as:
 The absence of or defective synthesis of R-protein, pancreatic proteases or intrinsic factor
 Surgical removal of much of the ileum or stomach
 Ileum diseases like Crohn’s disease
 Bacterial overgrowth in the intestine
 Tapeworm infestation
 Use of certain anti-reflux medications that significantly reduce acid production by the parietal cells (e.g.
Omiprazole)
 Chronic malabsorption syndromes which may occur with various gastrointestinal disorders
 Atrophic gastritis, which decreases the production of HCl and digestive enzymes, leading to inefficient
cleaving of R-protein and vitamin B-12 in foods. 10-30% of older adults have atrophic gastritis and thus
are advised to eat foods fortified with vitamin

2. The Folate Trap Hypothesis. Vitamin B-12 is involved in the conversion of cytoplasmic homocysteine

Consequence of vitamin B-12 deficiency: N5-methyl H4folate and homocysteine accumulate in the case of
vitamin-B12 deficiency, which in turn leads to deficiency for H4folate. The inability to ‘recycle’ N5-methyl
H4folate to H4folate effectively ‘traps’ intracellular pools of folate in the N5-methyl form. Even in the
presence of normal dietary supplies of folate, the failure of the B-12-mediated reaction to proceed will
continue to lead to the “trapping” of the N5-methyl derivative. Reactions that require other forms of folate,
such as those in purine and TMP synthesis, will be inhibited. Actively dividing cells, such as those of the
hematopoetic system, require a large pool of dNTPs as precursors for genomic replication. With folate
‘trapped’ in the N5-methyl form, the production of purines and TMP will be adversely affected. Cell
division rates will consequently be slowed, and the production of hematopoetic cells will be reduced. This
results in the characteristic macrocytic megaloblastic histology (enlarged, potentially multi-nucleated cells
that have failed to fully conclude cell division).
Sum result of vitamin B-12 deficiency: Vitamin B-12 deficiency thus may result in pernicious anemia,
exhibiting two types of symptoms: (i) hematopoetic (macrocytic anemia, with megaloblastic changes) and
(ii) neurological (peripheral neuropathy via progressive demyelination). While the anemia can be reversed
with either folate or B12 administration, if the neuropathy is severe, permanent loss of function can occur.
Prior to vitamin therapy, this disease was frequently fatal. Fortunately, dietary deficiency for vitamin B-12
is now quite rare in developed societies. One of the principal causes of pernicious anemia is an autoimmune
reaction against parietal cells of the stomach. Development of antibodies against an H+/K+-ATPase located
on the lumenal surface of parietal cells leads to infiltration of T and B cells into the submucosa and the
lamina, with degenerative changes in parietal cells. Antibodies against IF have also been occasionally
observed. The Schilling test is used for patients with vitamin B12 deficiency to determine whether the patient
has pernicious anemia.

Folic acid
Folic acid or folate plays a key role in one-carbon metabolism, transferring one-carbon fragments from
donors such as serine, glycine and histidine to intermediates in the synthesis of amino acids purines and
thymine. Folic acid is obtained primarily from yeasts and leafy vegetables as well as animal liver. The
folates are a collection of pteridine ring-based water soluble molecules. PABA (para-aminobenzoic acid) is
an intermediate in the synthesis of folic acid, which gets converted to dihydropteroic acid. This in turn gets
converted to dihydrofolic acid. Animal cannot synthesize PABA (para-aminobenzoic acid) nor attach
glutamate residues to pteroic acid, thus, requiring folate intake in the diet. When stored in the liver or
ingested, folic acid exists in a polyglutamate form. Intestinal mucosal cells remove some of the glutamate
residues through the action of the lysosomal enzyme, conjugase. The removal of glutamate residues makes
folate less negatively charged (from the polyglutamic acids) and therefore more capable of passing through
the basal lamenal membrane of the epithelial cells of the intestine and into the bloodstream. Folic acid is
reduced within cells (principally the liver where it is stored) to tetrahydrofolate (THF also H4folate) through
the action of dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), an NADPH-requiring enzyme.
Folic Acid Deficiency: A deficiency of folic acid will lead to megaloblastic anemia, due to the decreased
synthesis of purines and thymidine and resultant inability of cells to make DNA and divide. Note that
vitamin B-12 deficiency will also lead to the same consequences. Folate deficiency can also cause neural
tube defects in the fetus, again as a consequence of decreased DNA synthesis and cell division. Folic acid
was the most common vitamin deficiency in the US, particularly among pregnant women and alcoholics,
which led to supplementation of foods with this vitamin.

Pantothenic acid (Vitamin B-5)
Pantothenic acid is synthesized by green plants and most microorganisms. Pantothenic acid is a component
of coenzyme A, which functions in the transfer of acyl groups It is also a component of phosphopantetheine
in fatty acid synthase. Co A is required for reactions involving transfer of acyl groups, including the
metabolism of fats, carbohydrates, and proteins. More than 70 enzymes use CoA or CoA derivatives.
Deficiency is very rare because of the essential nature of CoA. No RDA has been established and no UL has
been established because there have been no adverse effects. Eggs, liver and yeast are the most important
sources of pantothenic acid although the vitamin is widely distributed.
Ascorbic (Vitamin C)
Ascorbic acid is more commonly known as vitamin C. Ascorbic acid is a dietary essential for human beings,
other primates, and the guinea pig, but can be synthesized by all other species of animals. The most
important reactions requiring ascorbate as a cofactor are those catalyzed by prolyl hydroxylase and lysyl
hydroxylase. This leads to the hydroxylation of proline, which is essential for collagen stability, and for
hydroxylysine residues involved in collagen cross-linking to form strong fibrils. Vitamin C is, therefore,
required for the maintenance of normal connective tissue as well as for wound healing and bone remodeling.
Deficiency in vitamin C: Vitamin C deficiency leads to the disease scurvy due to the role of the vitamin in
the post-translational modification of collagens. Scurvy is characterized by easily bruised skin, muscle
fatigue, soft swollen gums, decreased wound healing and hemorrhaging, osteoporosis, and anemia. Vitamin
C is readily absorbed and so the primary cause of vitamin C deficiency is poor diet, and this is rare today.

Other metabolic reactions requiring vitamin C as a cofactor include the catabolism of tyrosine and the
synthesis of epinephrine from tyrosine and the synthesis of the bile acids. Another function of ascorbate is as
a reducing agent in a number of different reactions, e.g. aiding in the intestinal absorption of iron by
reducing ferric iron to ferrous iron. It is involved in the biosynthesis of corticosteroids, aldosterone, the
conversion of cholesterol to bile acids and functions as a reducing agent for mixed-function oxidases.
Vitamin C is known for its antioxidant properties, and may act as a free radical scavenger which could play a
role in the prevention of certain chronic disorders, aging, etc.. Vitamin C is the most unstable of vitamins. It
is destroyed by long term storage and exposure to alkali. Megadoses of vitamin C have been suggested to
combat the common cold. However, controversy still surrounds this proposal.
Minerals
Minerals are inorganic substances, which are critical for body functions including cell metabolism, nerve
impulse transmission, growth and development as depicted in the figure given below. Based on their amount
present and required for proper functioning of body, minerals can be divided into two categories,
macrominerals and microminerals. There are eleven essential macrominerals: (i) sodium, potassium,
magnesium, calcium, phosphorus, and chloride and (ii) non-metallic elements in macronutrients like
proteins, and fats: hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and sulfur. The nine essential microminerals (trace
minerals) are iodine, iron, copper, zinc, manganese, molybdenum, selenium, chromium, fluoride. Some
books categorize cobalt as essential trace element, as it is a component of vitamin B12. However, in general,
the above nine are considered essential trace elements. Microminerals are also called as trace minerals as
these are generally present in most tissues at concentrations of less than 100 microgram/gram (100 ppm) or
required in the diet only in microgram or milligram quantities. Most important trace elements in clinical
nutrition are zinc, copper, selenium, iodine, iron and fluoride, as deficiency of these minerals is most
prevalent world-wide.

Zinc
With the exception of iron and iodine, essentiality of most trace minerals has only been recently recognized.
Intensive research on these began in early 1960s, with the discovery of a group of Middle-Eastern adolescent
boys who showed symptoms such as, dwarfism and undeveloped sexual organs. Later on, they were
diagnosed to be zinc deficient.
Why is zinc essential?
Zinc is an essential component of several enzymes including metalloenzymes and proteins. It is important
for healing, the sense of taste and smell, DNA synthesis and proper functioning of immune system, heme
synthesis, bone formation, growth and development during pregnancy, childhood and adolescence. Very
high doses of zinc reduced duration of cold symptoms in one study. Other studies have found no benefit, but
zinc lozenges are sold in many pharmacies. Because of the widespread deficiency of zinc in poor countries,
zinc supplements are given to young children to reduce morbidity and mortality from pneumonia & diarrhea.

Zinc deficiency
As zinc is component of several enzymes and proteins, it is logical to think that its deficiency will be
manifested at various physiological levels. Substantial evidence clearly indicates that the cell-mediated
immune system is adversely affected by zinc deficiency. Described below are important zinc deficiencies.




Acrodermatitis Enteropathica. It is a rare, autosomal recessive disorder, characterized by skin lesions,
diarrhea, alopecia, anorexia, growth impairment, delayed sexual maturation, and increased susceptibility
to infection. Persons with this disorder have very low plasma zinc concentrations (22 mcg/dl; normal is
70-120 mcg/dl). This condition is generally fatal if untreated. Treatment with sufficient oral zinc results
in rapid recovery.
Adolescent Zinc Deficiency. This is a clearly described syndrome, in which males are primarily
affected. Main symptoms include growth impairment, hypogonadism, delayed onset of puberty, failure of
development of secondary sexual characteristics. This condition responds well to oral zinc supplements.

Zinc Deficiency may also be associated with various chronic infectious diseases and malabsorption disorders
and may arise due to alcoholism. Zinc deficiency in alcoholics may be due to enhanced urinary excretion of
zinc as well as poor diet. Some studies in New Jersey, Alabama, and Chile demonstrated that maternal zinc
deficiency may be common and results in reduced fetal and neonatal growth.
Zinc toxicity
The UL for zinc is 40 mg/day. Ingesting amounts higher than 100 mg can result in diarrhea, cramps, nausea,
vomiting and decreased immune system function. Megadoses of zinc may also interfere with copper
absorption and metabolism, as these two minerals share a common protein, metallothionein, for their
absorption. Thus, high concentration of zinc competes with binding and absorption of copper. The UL of
zinc is specifically designed to avoid copper deficiency.

Copper
Why is copper essential?
The ability of copper to alternate between two oxidation states (Cu+ and Cu++) makes it important component
of many proteins such as, ceruloplasmin, which is involved in the oxidization of iron for its subsequent
transport to body cells. Low levels of this enzyme result in decreased synthesis of hemoglobin and
development of anemia. Therefore, copper and iron metabolism are closely linked. Copper is also part of
superoxide dismutase family, which prevents oxidative damage to the cells. Copper is involved in the
regulation of neurotransmitters and has an important role in connective tissue formation as a component of
lysyl oxidase. This enzyme cross-links the strands in elastin and collagen.
Absorption and metabolism of copper
Similar to zinc, copper is absorbed through small intestine, transported to blood cells, binds to albumin, and
then transported to liver and kidneys. Metallothionein is involved in the process as mentioned above. Liver is
the main storage site for copper, however very little copper is stored in the body. For transport from liver to
other tissues, copper is bound to ceruloplasmin, which binds to the specific receptors within the tissues and
releases copper. Excretion of copper is through bile into the feces.
Copper deficiency
Causes of copper deficiency include, (i) bariatric & peptic ulcer surgery, (ii) malabsorption diseases, (iii)
treatment with chelating agents such as EDTA, D-penicillamine used for the treatment of lead poisoning and
(iv) ingestion of high-dose Zn supplements or consumer products with high zinc levels. e.g. denture creams
or pennies. Sometimes it also occurs due to unknown reasons.
Recent studies show that copper deficiency may cause myelopathy and peripheral neuropathy with
symptoms and MRI results that resemble neuropathy due to vitamin B12 deficiency. The latter may co-exist
with copper deficiency. Anemia (mostly microcytic-small RBCs) and neutropenia co-exist with the
neurologic manifestations. Copper supplementation will correct the anemia and neutropenia, but may not
reverse the neuropathy. Copper deficiency is also manifested as Menkes Syndrome, a recessive, X-linked
trait, in which cellular copper transport is defective.
Copper toxicity
 Wilson's Disease. This is a rare autosomal recessive disorder. It is characterized by low plasma levels of
copper and ceruloplasmin and high tissue concentrations of copper and results in brain and liver lesions.
Treatment with D-penicillamine or high doses of zinc is effective. However, treatment with Dpenicillamine may lead to zinc deficiency.
 Hemodialysis. High concentrations of trace elements in contaminated dialysis fluids may have toxic
effects. Copper (also aluminum and zinc) toxicity is occasionally found in such patients.

Selenium
Why is selenium essential?
Selenium functions at the core of several selenoproteins, many of which are act as antioxidants, e.g. the
glutathione peroxidases (GPXs), thioredoxin reductases (TRs) and iodothyronine deiodinases (DIs). GPXs
for example, break down peroxides like hydrogen peroxide to water, thus preventing them from forming free
radicals and damaging cells. Other selenoproteins are needed for proper functioning of immune system and
thyroid gland. The deiodinase enzymes that convert thyroxine to triiodothyronine require selenium. There

=== PAGE 17 ===
are some studies which indicate that selenium may be beneficial in lowering the risk of prostate, colon and
other cancers. However, more research is needed in this area.
Selenium deficiency
Keshan disease. This is a congestive cardiomyopathy caused by dietary deficiency of selenium. It is named
after Keshan Providence in China, where symptoms were first noted. Later on it was found that this
condition is widespread in areas extending from northeast to southwest China, due to selenium-deficient soil.
This condition is often fatal and often affects children and women of child bearing age. It is characterized by
heart failure and pulmonary edema. This has now been largely eradicated by dietary selenium fortification.
Selenium toxicity
Plants growing in the soils containing high levels of soil can accumulate very high levels of selenium leading
to toxicity in the cattle and horses grazing on them such as loss of hair and weight, affected muscle function
and even death. It is said that selenium poisoning may have played a role in the late arrival of troops to the
battle of Little Bighorn. General George Custer was hoping for reinforcements, but a "peculiar sickness"
developed in the horses traveling across the plains, through what are now known to be high selenium
regions. Selenium toxicity symptoms in humans include nausea, diarrhea, fatigue, irritability and nervous
system disorders, hair loss, brittleness of nails, skin rashes and garlic breath odor.
Iodine
Why is iodine essential?
Iodine is needed for proper functioning of thyroid and production of thyroid hormones, thyroxine (T4) and
triiodothyronine (T3). These hormones play vital role in cell metabolism and are thus critical for regulation
of basal energy expenditure, macronutrient metabolism, growth and organ maturation. The thyroid gland
traps iodide from the blood stream and accumulates it for the synthesis of these hormones.
Iodine deficiency
Iodine deficiency is the second most prevalent trace element deficiency in the world. Deficiency causes
impaired growth, cognitive impairment, goiter (enlarged thyroid), and other problems. If there is a deficiency
in iodine, the pituitary gland secretes TSH, thyroid-stimulating hormone. The thyroid gland thus enlarges
(goiter) in response to the increased levels of TSH, trying to remove as much iodide as possible from the
bloodstream. It is important to note that enlarged thyroid gland can also be due to certain diseases or
conditions not related to iodine intake. Fetus is especially susceptible to iodine deficiency as it can lead to
conditions such as low birth weight, neurological disorders, and impaired mental function. The restriction of
brain development and growth is called as cretinism. This is characterized by short stature, severe cognitive
impairment etc.
Iodine toxicity
High intake of iodine was reported especially in certain parts of Japan due to high consumption of iodinerich seaweeds. High intake of iodine may increase the risk of papillary cancer.

Iron
Why is iron essential?
Iron is component of important proteins such as hemoglobin, myoglobin, and cytochromes. Hemoglobin is
present in red blood cells and carries oxygen to tissues, myoglobin is present in muscles and controls oxygen
uptake from red blood cells, while cytochromes are group of proteins that carry electrons during certain

=== PAGE 18 ===
reactions involved in release of energy from macronutrients. Thus, well-functioning of all these systems is
necessary to provide energy to the cells to perform work; absence of which leads to fatigue, a major
symptom of iron deficiency.
Iron deficiency
Iron deficiency is the most prevalent deficiency in the world. Iron deficiency is a fairly common problem in
premenopausal women, while it is least in elderly people (hence the absence of iron in ‘silver’
supplements).
Iron deficiency often exists without anemia, but deficiency without anemia will progress to anemia
(microcytic) if causes of deficiency are not corrected. In microcytic anemia, red blood cells are small due to
insufficient hemoglobin production. Some of the main symptoms of iron deficiency include:
 Fatigue, reduced work capacity and exercise tolerance
 Sensation of feeling cold
 Affected neurotransmitter function, and immunologic and inflammatory defenses
 Pica (compulsive eating of nonfood items)
 Pagophagia (compulsive eating of ice)
 Pallor, pale conjuctiva and spoon nails
Iron toxicity
Iron also poses a risk if consumed in excess, thus, upper level of 45 mg/day has been set for iron. Intakes
above this level, especially from supplements and highly fortified foods can cause nausea, vomiting, stomach
irritation, diarrhea and impaired absorption of other minerals. In United States, accidental iron overdose is
the leading cause of poisoning in the children under the age of six years. Adult overload may also result from
frequent blood transfusions.
Hemochromatosis (primary) is a genetic condition that is responsible for iron overload in adults, more
prevalent in men than women, usually diagnosed after age 35. Symptoms are not specific and may include
impotence, severe fatigue, bronze or gray skin, chest pain, abdominal pain, and depression. The normal
degradation of transport protein is not efficient in this condition, leading to excess absorption and transport
of iron to tissues leading to overload of iron and tissue injury, deposits in liver, heart and other organs,
eventually liver disease and heat failure. Treatment with periodic phlebotomy to decrease iron stores is very
effective for this condition. Also avoid vitamins with iron or vitamin C which promotes iron absorption, and
avoid alcohol if there is liver damage. Secondary hemochromatosis occurs due to excess intake of iron.

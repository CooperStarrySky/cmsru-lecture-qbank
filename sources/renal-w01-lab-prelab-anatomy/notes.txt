Retroperitoneum & Posterior Abdominal Wall
Review: What is the peritoneum?
•
•
•

The peritoneum lines the walls of the abdominal and pelvic cavities
Composed of a double layer of serous membrane (like pleura and pericardium):
o Parietal peritoneum (superficial)
o Visceral peritoneum (deep layer; covers viscera)
Secretes serous fluid to allow for movement of visceral organs

Terms of relationship to the peritoneum
• Visceral organs can be classified by their relationship to the peritoneum:
o Intraperitoneal: structures within the peritoneum
o Retroperitoneal: structures posterior to (outside) the peritoneum
o Subperitoneal: structures inferior to the peritoneum
• Examples:
o Major blood vessels: RETROPERITONEAL
o Kidneys, adrenal glands, ureters: RETROPERITONEAL
o Bladder, uterus: SUBPERITONEAL

Muscles of the Posterior Abdominal Wall
* Note: You do not need to know muscle attachment sites for these
Quadratus lumborum muscle
• Action: contributes to spinal stability
o The above action is sufficient at this point in your education, but this muscle has a more
nuanced action. When both contract (bilateral contraction), QL aids in spinal extension. When
one contracts (unilateral contraction), QL aids in ipsilateral spinal flexion
• You don’t need to memorize the innervation, but for the sake of completeness, this muscle is
innervated by the subcostal nerve (T12), and the ventral (anterior) rami of spinal nerves L1-L4
Iliacus
• Action: thigh flexion
• Innervation: femoral nerve (L2-L4)
Psoas major
• Action: thigh flexion
• Innervation: L1-L3

ask about it. This muscle is present in approximately 50-60% of people. Its action is that it contributes weakly
to hip flexion and lateral flexion of the trunk. It is innervated by the ventral ramus (anterior) of spinal nerve L1

Kidney Anatomy
Kidney location
•
•
•

Remember that kidneys are retroperitoneal (posterior to the peritoneum) and they are enclosed in
perirenal fat
Left kidney – superior pole is anterior to ribs 11 and 12
Right kidney – superior pole is anterior to rib 12 (lower than the left due to presence of the liver)
6

Kidney anatomy
•
•
•
•
•
•

Each kidney is covered in a fibrous renal capsule
Renal medulla is composed of renal columns and renal pyramids
Renal pyramids collect and transport urine through nephrons (functional unit of kidney; not visible) and
exit renal pyramids via renal papillae
Multiple minor calyces converge on larger major calyces (sing. Calyx), these funnel urine into the
renal pelvis
Urine then passes into the ureter and then the bladder
Renal hilum– opening on medial side of kidney for renal vessels, lymphatics, and nerves to enter/exit
kidney
o Renal hilum contents listed posterior to anterior: (helpful for siding an isolated kidney!)
 Ureter
 Renal artery
 Renal vein

Suprarenal Glands
•
•

On the superior pole of each kidney is a suprarenal (adrenal) gland
o Right suprarenal gland: pyramidal shaped
o Left suprarenal gland: crescent shaped
Note: suprarenal glands are not attached to the kidneys! They sit on top (like little hats)

Ureter & Bladder Anatomy
Ureter
•
•

Ureters are a continuation of the renal pelvis and descend through the abdomen, into the pelvis, and
terminate at the bladder
Function: Transport urine to the bladder

Ureter constriction sites
Ureters have three natural constriction sites, which are susceptible to blockage by renal caculi:
1. Ureteropelvic junction: between the ureter and the renal pelvis
2. Crossing over the external iliac artery
3. Ureterovesical junction: junction between the ureter and the bladder
Ureters: Blood supply
The ureters receive arterial branches from adjacent vessels as they travel toward the bladder:
• Upper ureter from renal arteries
• Middle ureter from abdominal aorta, gonadal arteries, and common iliac arteries
• Lower (pelvic) ureter from internal iliac arteries

Bladder
Bladder location
• The bladder is the most anterior of the visceral organs in the pelvis
• Located just posterior to the pubic symphysis and anterior the uterus (if present) and rectum
Bladder anatomy
• Detrusor muscle – smooth muscle located in the walls of the urinary bladder
• Internal bladder features:
o Trigone – triangular area located in the posterior wall of the bladder. Formed by three openings:
 Ureteric openings (2)
 Internal urethral orifice (1)

Innervation
Innervation: Kidneys
Kidney Sympathetics - Sympathetic innervation controls vascular tone within the kidney (vasoconstriction)
and decreases glomerular filtration (urine output decreases)
• Pre-ganglionic fibers travel along Least splanchnic nerves (T12)
• Pre-ganglionic fibers synapse within the aorticorenal ganglia (located on the aorta near the renal aa)
• Post-ganglionic fibers continue to the kidneys
Kidney Parasympathetics - Parasympathetic has minimal function and its role is unclear in the kidney
• Vagus nerves (CN X) – preganglionic cell bodies located in the brainstem
• Pre-ganglionic fibers travel through plexuses and ganglia to the kidneys
• Pre-ganglionic fibers will synapse on intrinsic ganglia within the kidneys

Innervation: Ureters
Ureter Sympathetics - Sympathetic innervation controls vascular tone within the ureters
• Source of innervation varies depending on region of the ureter
• Pre-ganglionic fibers arise from spinal levels T11-L2 and synapse within the ganglia of the sympathetic
chain
• Post-ganglionic fibers are distributed via multiple plexuses depending on the portion of the ureter
Ureter Parasympathetics - Parasympathetic innervation promotes peristalsis within the ureter
• Upper and middle ureter: from Vagus nerves (CN X)
• Lower (pelvic) ureter: from Pelvic splanchnic nerves (S2-S4)
• Pre-ganglionic fibers travel through plexuses and ganglia to ureters and synapse on intrinsic ganglia

Innervation: Visceral sensory (aﬀerent)- kidneys & ureters
Kidney & ureter visceral sensory (afferent) innervation
• Visceral pain fibers travel along sympathetic pathway (T11-L2)
• Pain is referred to the cutaneous areas innervated by the same
spinal levels
• Ureteral pain due to a stone traveling down from the renal pelvis
to the bladder is described as “loin to groin” pain

Clinical correlation: Kidney stones (Renal calculi)
Renal calculi – (aka. Kidney stones) polycrystalline aggregates
of calcium, phosphate, urate, and other soluble salts.
• When urine becomes saturated with these salts, variations
in pH cause the salts to precipitate and form calculi.
• Passing kidney stone can be painful and often presents as
restlessness, hematuria, burning sensations during
urination, vomiting, and intense lower back pain.
• Small stones may pass, but if they are larger than the
ureter, they may become lodged
• Pain projects to T11 - L2 spinal segments and is typically
referred to the lower back and groin via the T11 - L2
dermatomes as the stone passes through the ureter.

Innervation: Suprarenal Glands
Sympathetic only!
• Pre-ganglionic sympathetic fibers originate from T10-L1 lateral horns and travel via the greater, lesser,
and least splanchnic nerves
• Sympathetic fibers have two different destinations and, therefore, have two different functions:
1. Suprarenal vasculature - Sympathetic innervation controls vascular tone within suprarenal glands
• Pre-ganglionic fibers synapse in celiac ganglion
• Post-ganglionic fibers make their way to the suprarenal aa via periarterial plexuses
2. Suprarenal medulla - Sympathetic activity promotes secretion of epinephrine and norepinephrine
• Pre-ganglionic fibers pass through the celiac ganglion without synapsing
• Pre-ganglionic fibers instead make their way to the suprarenal medulla and directly synapse on
the chromaffin cells

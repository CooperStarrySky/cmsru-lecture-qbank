Neuroendocrine tumors of the GI tract (GNETs)
Gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (GNET) arise from neuroendocrine cells present within
the stomach, small intestine, and pancreas. GNETs are well-differentiated tumors which are frequently
indolent and have variable biologic behavior. These include carcinoid tumors and pancreatic islet cell
tumors (gastrinoma, somatostatinoma, insulinoma, glucagonoma, and VIPoma).
GNETs are uncommon. In the United States, GNETs combined have a prevalence of 25 per 1,000,000
population. Approximately half are carcinoids. GNETs may occur sporadically or as part of an autosomal
dominant inherited multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) syndrome. All gastrointestinal endocrine tumors
can be associated with MEN type 1 which is characterized by pituitary adenomas, parathyroid
hyperplasia, and pancreatic tumors.

GNETs are of neuroendocrine origin; they may secrete various peptides and hormones. Most of these
tumors produce several hormones, but very few are associated with a clinical syndrome. They also
produce substances including chromogranins, which can be localized with immunochemical studies.
Carcinoid tumors and carcinoid syndrome
Carcinoid tumors are slow growing and can occur anywhere in the alimentary tract. The clinical
presentation varies from an asymptomatic incidental finding to symptomatic tumors, including the classic
carcinoid syndrome.

Most carcinoid tumors are found incidentally. If symptoms are present, they often are nonspecific and
associated with the location and extent of the tumor. Symptoms due to the direct effects of a tumor in the
gastrointestinal tract may be abdominal pain, intestinal obstruction, nausea, weight loss, or intestinal
bleeding. Carcinoid tumors are rare, and those that cause carcinoid syndrome are even rarer. Only 5% of
patients with carcinoid tumors have carcinoid syndrome. Carcinoid syndrome is due to peptides released
by the tumor into the systemic circulation including histamine, serotonin, prostaglandins, and tachykinins.
The liver often inactivates these peptides; thus, patients have symptoms of carcinoid syndrome primarily
in association with liver metastases: the bioactive products are secreted directly into the hepatic veins. The
most common symptoms of carcinoid syndrome are facial flushing and diarrhea. Flushes can be
associated with hypotension and tachycardia. Several inciting factors are known for the flushing
associated with carcinoid syndrome: alcohol ingestion, increased emotional states, trauma or pressure on
the liver. Anesthesia can provoke long episodes of flushing with life-threatening hypotension known as
carcinoid crisis. Carcinoid crisis can be prevented by the administration of octreotide (a somatostatin
analogue) before anesthesia. A secretory diarrhea occurs in 80% of patients with carcinoid syndrome and
can be quite severe. Wheezing is a common component of carcinoid syndrome and is due to
bronchospasm and right-sided valvular heart disease. Unlike diarrhea, wheezing and dyspnea are worse
during flushing episodes. Of importance, wheezing associated with carcinoid syndrome should not be
treated like bronchial asthma: treatment with β- agonists can incite prolonged vasodilation and severe
hypotension.
Gastric carcinoid tumors tend to occur in the body of the stomach. They occur more frequently in patients
who have a disease that causes hypergastrinemia, such as pernicious anemia and atrophic gastritis with
achlorhydria. They also appear to be more common in patients with Zollinger-Ellison syndrome. Most
gastric carcinoids are small, indolent, and have low malignant potential. Rarely, sporadic carcinoid tumors
arise in the absence of hypergastrinemia. These behave in an aggressive fashion including liver metastases
and carcinoid syndrome due to 5-hydroxytryptophan production.
Small intestinal carcinoid tumors are the most important clinically because patients are more likely to
present with intestinal symptoms and carcinoid syndrome. Abdominal pain or bowel obstruction can be
caused by the direct mechanical effect of the tumor or mesenteric ischemia due to tumor-associated
fibrosis. Most small intestinal carcinoids occur in the distal ileum. Because small intestinal carcinoids,
regardless of size, have the potential to metastasize, they should be removed surgically.
Up to half of carcinoids are appendiceal tumors, and carcinoid tumors are the most common neoplasms of
the appendix. Incidental carcinoids are found in 0.5% of appendectomy specimens. They usually are
small, solitary, and benign.
Most carcinoid tumors are found incidentally on endoscopy or imaging studies performed for other
indications. If symptoms are present and are due to local effects of the tumor, they usually are found on
CT. If symptoms of carcinoid syndrome are strongly suspected, the best initial evaluation is with urinary
5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) and/or a serum serotonin. Octreotide scintigraphy identifies the site
of primary tumors and metastatic disease in more than 80% of patients with carcinoid syndrome.
Gastrinoma
Gastrinomas produce the classic triad of symptoms called Zollinger-Ellison syndrome. This syndrome
consists of peptic ulcer disease, gastric acid hypersecretion, and a gastrin-producing tumor. Gastrinomas
are rare and occur in less than 1% of patients who have peptic ulcer disease. Gastrinomas are frequently
associated with MEN 1 syndrome.

More than 90% of gastrinomas occur in an anatomical area called the gastrinoma triangle (bounded by
the proximal duodenum and pancreatic neck). Gastrinomas are slow growing, and it can be difficult to
differentiate benign tumors from malignant ones. Approximately two-thirds of gastrinomas are malignant.
The best indicator of malignancy is the presence of metastases, which most often affect the regional
lymph nodes or the liver.
Peptic ulcer disease is the most common sign of gastrinoma and occurs in more than 90% of patients. The
ulcer disease is characterized by multiple post-bulbar duodenal ulcers and severe refractory esophagitis.
Also, 50% of the patients have diarrhea due to the effect of acid hypersecretion on the small bowel.
Increased acid exposure to the small bowel causes morphologic and inflammatory changes that can result
in malabsorption. In addition, the low pH may inactivate pancreatic lipase and cause bile salts to
precipitate, resulting in malabsorption of fat and steatorrhea. If a patient has duodenal ulcers that are not
caused by either H. pylori infection or NSAIDs and chronic diarrhea, gastrinoma should be considered.
For patients with suspected of gastrinoma, the first screening test is determining the serum level of gastrin
(which must be performed off acid suppressing medications). A serum gastrin level greater than 1,000
pg/mL suggests the presence of gastrinoma. A level exceeding 110 pg/mL but less than1,000 pg/mL may
be consistent with several conditions that cause hypergastrinemia. The most common cause of
hypergastrinemia is achlorhydria due to atrophic gastritis. Other causes of achlorhydria include gastric
carcinoma, vagotomy, or current proton pump inhibitor therapy. Some disorders cause hypergastrinemia
with normal or increased acid secretion. These are gastric outlet obstruction, retained gastric antrum in
patients with previous gastric surgery, and a rare hereditary condition called antral G-cell hyperplasia.
If the gastrin level is increased, then it must be determined whether there is concomitant gastric acid
hypersecretion. This is done by measuring basal acid output (BAO). A nasogastric tube is placed into the
antrum, and samples of gastric fluid are collected; a BAO greater than 15 mEq/hour is diagnostic of
Zollinger-Ellison syndrome. If a patient has pronounced hypergastrinemia and acid hypersecretion but the
serum gastrin level is below 1,000 pg/mL, an intravenous secretin test is indicated. In patients with
Zollinger- Ellison syndrome, the serum level of gastrin increases at least 200 pg/mL over the basal gastrin
level. Patients with other causes of hypergastrinemic hyperchlorhydria have only a slight or no increase in
the serum level of gastrin.
Once there is biochemical evidence of gastrinoma, the tumor should be localized. Because most
gastrinomas occur in the gastrinoma triangle, EUS is very sensitive in localizing the primary tumor but is
less helpful in evaluating metastatic disease. Most gastrinomas have somatostatin receptors. Octreotide
scintigraphy can localize 85% of gastrinomas.
Surgical resection is the treatment of choice for patients with resectable disease. Patients with MEN 1
syndrome may not be candidates for surgical treatment because they often have multiple tumors. If
resection is not possible, the objective in treating Zollinger-Ellison syndrome is to control gastric acid
hypersecretion. Medical treatment to decrease gastric acid hypersecretion usually consists of proton pump
inhibitors and octreotide.
Insulinoma
Insulinomas are insulin-secreting islet cell tumors that originate in the pancreas and cause symptoms of
hypoglycemia. They are usually solitary but, rarely, may be multiple. Most patients present with clinical
manifestations of hypoglycemia. Symptoms may also result from catecholamine release caused by

hypoglycemia. These symptoms are anxiety, weakness, fatigue, headache, palpitations, tremor, and
sweating. Typically, symptoms occur with fasting, when a meal is delayed, or during exercise. Patients
learn to avoid symptoms by eating frequently; as a result, 40% of patients have a history of weight gain.
The presence of an insulinoma is determined by the combination of a low fasting blood glucose level and
an inappropriately increased plasma insulin level. This combination is identified in 65% of patients with
insulinoma. For a definitive diagnosis, a 72-hour fast is required, with serum glucose and insulin levels
determined at regular intervals and with the onset of symptoms. With this fasting test, symptoms develop
in virtually 100% within 72 hours. Localization of the tumor can be difficult because most tumors are
small. Because it is less common for insulinomas to have somatostatin receptors, octreotide scintigraphy
can localize only 50% of the tumors and therefore is not commonly used. These tumors are almost
exclusively in the pancreas, so EUS can detect nearly 90% of them.
Definitive treatment of insulinoma is surgical removal of the tumor. Patients with metastatic disease and
those with insulinomas that have not been removed can be managed with hyperglycemic agents such as
diazoxide and octreotide.
VIPoma
VIPoma syndrome is caused by a neuroendocrine tumor that is usually in the pancreas and produces
vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP). This syndrome is characterized by severe watery diarrhea,
hypokalemia, and achlorhydria and is also known as the WDHA syndrome (watery diarrhea, hypokalemia,
and achlorhydria), also known as Verner-Morrison Syndrome.
A VIPoma usually is a solitary pancreatic islet cell tumor, and more than 75% of them occur in the body
or tail of the pancreas. Although these tumors are slow growing, they frequently reach a large size before
diagnosis. Seventy-five percent of VIPomas are malignant, and 50% have metastasized at the time of
diagnosis. VIPomas cannot be differentiated from other pancreatic endocrine tumors with conventional
histologic examination. Demonstration of immunoreactive VIP in the tumor and plasma establishes the
diagnosis. VIP induces intestinal water and chloride secretion and inhibits gastric acid secretion.
VIPomas cause secretory diarrhea, which results in hypokalemia and dehydration. Stool volume may
exceed 5 L/day. The watery diarrhea resembles that of cholera (“pancreatic cholera”). Flushing of the
head and trunk may occur in some patients. Also, some patients develop hyperglycemia because of VIPand hypokalemia-induced glycogenolysis in the liver.
VIPoma syndrome should be suspected if patients present with high-volume watery diarrhea that persists
despite fasting and is associated with hypokalemia and dehydration. The diagnosis is confirmed by the
finding of an increased plasma concentration of VIP. Because these tumors are large, frequently
malignant, and metastatic, CT or MRI is effective for localizing the tumor and demonstrating metastatic
disease.
The first priority of treatment is to correct the dehydration and electrolyte abnormalities. Patients may
require 5 L or more of fluid per day, with aggressive potassium replacement. Long-acting octreotide
controls the diarrhea in most patients with VIPoma. Surgery should be considered for all patients without
evidence of metastatic disease. Surgical resection of a pancreatic VIPoma relieves all symptoms and is
curative in approximately 30% of patients.

Glucogonomas
Glucagonomas are solitary, large tumors which produce a rare syndrome of dermatitis, glucose
intolerance, weight loss, and anemia associated with a pancreatic islet cell tumor. Sixty-five percent of the
tumors are located in the head of the pancreas, and the other 35% occur equally in the body and tail. Most
tumors are metastatic at the time of diagnosis.
The characteristic presentation is that of a distinct dermatitis called necrolytic migratory erythema. This
rash starts as an erythematous area which spreads laterally and then become raised, with superficial
central blistering or bullous formation. When the bullae rupture, crusting occurs and the lesions begin to
heal from the center with subsequent hyperpigmented skin. The entire sequence usually takes 1 to 2
weeks and consists of a mixed pattern of erythema, bullous formation, epidermal separation, crusting, and
hyperpigmentation, which wax and wane. Glossitis, angular stomatitis, dystrophic nails, and hair thinning
are other clinical findings. The majority of patients with glucagonoma also have hypoaminoacidemia,
which may be responsible for the rash. Glucagon stimulates glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, lipolysis,
and ketogenesis. Most patients have glucose intolerance, and some may have frank diabetes mellitus.
Most patients with glucagonoma also have noticeable weight loss, even if the tumor is found incidentally
and is small.
If the clinical features of glucagonoma are present, the diagnosis can be confirmed by the finding of an
increased plasma glucagon level greater than 1,000 pg/mL. Because glucagonomas occur in the pancreas
and tend to be large and metastatic at the time of clinical presentation, CT of the abdomen usually
localizes the tumor.
The initial objective of treatment is to control the symptoms and hyperglycemia and to restore nutritional
status. Patients should receive nutritional support, and the hyperglycemia should be corrected. The rash
may improve with correction of the hypoaminoacidemia. Octreotide is useful for controlling symptoms,
and it improves the dermatitis, weight loss, diarrhea, and abdominal pain but not diabetes mellitus.
Surgery is curative in only 20% of patients but these tumors are slow growing and survival is good even
for those who do not receive chemotherapy.
Somatostatinoma
Somatostatinomas are neuroendocrine tumors that occur in the pancreas and intestine. Somatostatinomas
are the least common of the GNETs. They are usually solitary and large, and the most have metastasized
at the time of diagnosis. Somatostatin inhibits insulin release, gallbladder motility, and the secretion of
pancreatic enzymes and bicarbonate. They produce a distinct syndrome of diabetes mellitus, gallbladder
disease, and steatorrhea.
Most somatostatinomas are found incidentally when laparotomy is performed for gallbladder disease. The
diagnosis is established by the finding of somatostatin-containing D cells in the resected tumor and an
increased plasma concentration of somatostatin-like immunoreactive material.
Diabetes mellitus usually is mild and responds to oral hypoglycemic agents or low doses of insulin.
Surgical excision is the treatment of choice, but most patients present with metastatic disease.
Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1)
MEN1 syndrome has been classically characterized by the presence of neoplasms in the pituitary,
parathyroid, and pancreas although patients with MEN1 syndrome have been reported with a varying
combination of more than 20 endocrine and nonendocrine tumors. MEN1 syndrome occurs both as a

familial (90%) and sporadic form. While some neuroendocrine tumors (mainly gastrinomas) and foregut
carcinoids are often malignant, the majority of tumors in MEN1 are benign.
Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is the main MEN1-associated endocrinopathy occurring in 90% of
individuals and manifests as hypercalcemia in 100% of affected individuals by age 50 years. MEN1
parathyroid disease is a multiglandular disorder with enlargement of all the parathyroid glands.
Most pituitary tumors in MEN1 syndrome are represented by macroadenomas. Pituitary tumors may
occur as the first clinical manifestation of MEN1 syndrome. Pituitary tumors are usually functioning
tumors and prolactinoma is the most common among them, manifesting as oligomenorrhea/amenorrhea
and galactorrhea in women and sexual dysfunction and gynecomastia in men.
Neuroendocrine tumors of the GI tract and pancreas include gastrinoma, insulinoma, glucagonoma, or
VIPoma. MEN1-gastrinomas usually include a malignant component and in approximately 50% of
subjects with MEN1 syndrome has metastasized before diagnosis. MEN1-insulinomas are almost always
benign. Nonsecreting GNETs are frequent based on endoscopic ultrasonographic evaluation of patients
with MEN1. Gastric carcinoids occur approximately in 10% of individuals with MEN1 syndrome and are
often found incidentally.
MEN1 syndrome is an autosomal disorder resulting from mutations of the MEN1 gene which encodes a
novel 610-amino acid protein, referred to as menin. More than 1000 different germline MEN1 mutations
have been identified. Most mutations in the MEN1 gene predict truncation or absence of encoded menin.
The ultimate pathways of menin action in normal tissues or in tumors remain unknown. Menin is mainly
located in the nucleus and contains nuclear localization domains essential for the regulation of gene
expression. Menin is widely expressed and may play different roles in different tissues. Based on
chromosomal localization studies and its ability to interact with various nuclear proteins such as p21 and
p53, it is likely involved in the physiological regulation of several cell functions, including DNA
replication and repair.
MEN1 syndrome is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. Although approximately 90% of
individuals diagnosed with MEN1 syndrome have an affected parent, the family history may seem to be
negative because of failure to recognize the disorder in family members or late onset of the disease in the
affected parent. The penetrance of this syndrome approaches 100% with increasing age. Variable
expression is frequently observed among members from different MEN1 families sharing a common
MEN1 mutation. The great diversity of MEN1 mutations and a lack of genotype–phenotype correlation,
make mutational screening difficult and expensive. Clinical assessment and biochemical testing may be
used for surveillance of patients known to have MEN1 on genetic and/or clinical basis and for individuals
at 50% risk of having MEN1 syndrome whose genetic status is unknown.
As early diagnosis of MEN1 syndrome-associated tumors, and treatment of metabolic complications of
MEN1 has eliminated ZES and/or complicated PHPT as causes of death, management of diabetes and
screening for MEN1 syndrome-associated malignancies have assumed greater importance. Mutation
positive individuals should undergo a focused surveillance for early identification of potentially malignant
neuroendocrine tumors.

Vascular disorders of the GI tract
Mesenteric ischemia
Mesenteric ischemia can occur from any of the myriad of conditions that decrease intestinal blood flow.
Vascular disease of the esophagus is extremely rare. The stomach, duodenum, and rectum have numerous
arterial inputs with rich collateralization. Vascular disorders affecting the stomach, duodenum, or rectum
are extremely rare. The principal arterial supply to the gut distal to the esophagus is from the celiac,
superior mesenteric, and inferior mesenteric arteries. There are extensive collaterals between these
vessels. They enlarge rapidly in response to localized mesenteric ischemia. The inferior mesenteric vein
joins the splenic vein, which in turn joins the superior mesenteric vein to form the portal vein.
Ischemic colitis
Ischemic colitis represents nearly one-half of all cases of mesenteric ischemia. Atherosclerotic or
thrombotic occlusion of the inferior mesenteric artery or its branches and nonocclusive low-flow states are
usual causes of ischemic colitis. Less common causes include embolus, vasculitis, hypercoagulable states,
and colonic obstruction. Unusual associations include long-distance running, use of birth control pills,
cocaine, or sumatriptan. Some gastrointestinal infections and chronic inflammatory bowel disease can
mimic ischemic colitis. Ischemic colitis due to low-flow states often affects watershed areas such as the
splenic flexure, descending colon, and the right colon. The rectum is often spared in ischemic colitis. The
clinical symptoms of ischemic colitis include acute abdominal pain, urgency, diarrhea, and GI bleeding.
Physical findings often include abdominal tenderness over the affected bowel, distention, fever, and
tachycardia. Radiographs of the abdomen may show submucosal edema and hemorrhage
(“thumbprinting”). Colonoscopy findings consistent with ischemic colitis include segmental ulceration,
edema, and submucosal hemorrhage. CT scan may exclude other disorders. Typically, angiography is not
required, but should be used for patients with right-sided colonic ischemia as they may also have small
bowel ischemia. Treatment depends on the cause and includes supportive treatment, broad-spectrum
antibiotics, and, in selected cases, surgery if there are signs and symptoms of transmural necrosis. In most
patients, ischemic colitis resolves promptly with supportive therapy alone. Patients with idiopathic
ischemic colitis should be evaluated for thrombophilic states.

Acute mesenteric ischemia
Patients with acute mesenteric ischemia involving the small bowel often present with abdominal pain that
is severe, persistent, and poorly localized. The pain typically is more severe than the findings on
abdominal palpation (pain out of proportion to the exam). Prompt evaluation is critical.
Superior Mesenteric Artery (SMA) Embolus
Superior mesenteric artery emboli are common, accounting for 5% of peripheral emboli and 50% of cases
of primary mesenteric ischemia of the small bowel. The emboli are usually from the heart. Arrhythmias,
myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, and age older than 50 years are major risk factors.
Embolectomy is the treatment of choice. Patients with acute occlusion of a distal branch of the superior
mesenteric artery may be treated with thrombolytic therapy, intra-arterial papaverine, or anticoagulation.
Vasoconstriction of the superior mesenteric artery often occurs after occlusion and persists after
embolectomy. Intra-arterial papaverine 24 hours after embolectomy improves arterial flow.
Anticoagulation reduces the risk of further embolization.

Superior Mesenteric Artery (SMA) Thrombus
Superior mesenteric artery thrombus accounts for about 15% of cases of primary mesenteric small bowel
ischemia. Risk factors for superior mesenteric artery thrombus include old age, low-flow states
(arrhythmia, hypotension, sepsis, myocardial infarction, and congestive heart failure), atherosclerosis,
vasculitis, hypercoagulable states, and abdominal aortic aneurysm. Therapy usually involves surgical
thrombectomy or surgical bypass grafting, and resection of necrotic bowel.
Nonocclusive Mesenteric Ischemia
Nonocclusive mesenteric ischemia accounts for 20% of cases of acute mesenteric ischemia of the small
bowel. Low-flow states, vasospasm (cocaine), and preexisting atherosclerotic disease are risk factors.
Diagnosis is made by angiography. Treatment involves optimization of cardiac output, avoidance of
vasospastic medications, and prolonged selective intra-arterial infusion of vasodilators. Anticoagulation
generally is not necessary but broad-spectrum antibiotics should be administered.
Mesenteric Venous Thrombosis
Mesenteric venous thrombosis, usually superior mesenteric vein thrombosis (up to 95% of cases),
accounts for about 5% to 10% of cases of acute mesenteric ischemia. Risk factors include a history of
deep venous thrombosis, hypercoagulable states, intra-abdominal inflammation, malignant obstruction,
portal hypertension, and trauma. Symptoms may be acute or chronic and include abdominal pain,
anorexia, altered bowel habits, abdominal distention, and gastrointestinal tract bleeding. Because the
differential diagnosis is broad and includes other causes of acute abdominal pain, the initial evaluation
usually involves contrast-enhanced CT which is frequently diagnostic of mesenteric venous thrombosis.
Therapy involves fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and anticoagulation. Mesenteric venous
thrombosis may be an incidental CT finding in patients with portal hypertension, chronic pancreatitis, or
malignancy. Long-term anticoagulation should be considered unless there is a contraindication.
Chronic mesenteric ischemia
Patients with chronic mesenteric ischemia present with episodic ischemic abdominal pain that typically is
postprandial and becomes worse with time. Patients lose weight because of fear of eating (sitophobia).
Arteriography of these patients usually shows atherosclerotic stenosis of the origin of at least two of the
three major visceral arteries. A history of previous vascular disease, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, renal
insufficiency, and smoking is common. Some patients may have malabsorption, unexplained
gastroduodenal ulcerations, and small-bowel villous atrophy. A bruit on abdominal examination is
common. CT angiography and magnetic resonance imaging may be useful in the identification of
proximal large vessel arterial lesions. Surgical reconstruction or angioplasty with or without stents can be
therapeutic.

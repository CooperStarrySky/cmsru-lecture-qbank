Renal circulation and the filtration barrier
The glomerulus filters plasma into Bowman’s space. These notes trace the flow of blood and filtrate, explain
what sets glomerular filtration rate (GFR), and show how arteriolar resistance and feedback stabilize filtration.

Two capillary beds in series
The kidneys receive about 20–25% of resting cardiac output (roughly 1.0–1.1 L/min of blood).
Afferent arteriole → glomerular capillaries → efferent arteriole → peritubular capillaries or vasa recta.
Relatively high glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure favors filtration. Lower downstream capillary
hydrostatic pressure and the rise in plasma oncotic pressure after filtration favor uptake of reabsorbed fluid.
A GFR of 125 mL/min yields approximately 180 L of filtrate per day. The tubules reclaim most of that volume,
and urine volume varies with water intake and losses. Filtration and tubular transport support waste excretion,
fluid and electrolyte balance, acid–base regulation, and blood pressure control. The kidneys also produce
erythropoietin and renin and contribute to hormone metabolism and gluconeogenesis.

Nephron anatomy
Filtrate travels from Bowman’s space through the proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal convoluted tubule, and
connecting tubule into the collecting system. Cortical nephrons have superficial glomeruli and shorter loops.
Juxtamedullary nephrons have deep cortical glomeruli, long loops, and associated vasa recta. Long loops help
establish the medullary osmotic gradient. The vasa recta help preserve it through countercurrent exchange.

Three layers of the filtration barrier
The renal corpuscle includes the glomerular capillary tuft and Bowman’s capsule. The parietal epithelium lines
the capsule, and visceral podocytes cover the capillaries. Mesangial cells support the tuft and can influence the
surface area available for filtration.

Regulation of renal blood flow and GFR
Slides 19–25

Myogenic autoregulation
Increased perfusion pressure stretches afferent arteriolar smooth muscle. Stretch-induced depolarization
promotes Ca²⁺ entry through voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels, which increases tone and limits the rise in glomerular
pressure and flow. A fall in pressure reduces stretch and favors dilation. Autoregulation buffers changes over a
range of perfusion pressures, with limits that depend on physiological and clinical context.

Tubuloglomerular feedback
The juxtaglomerular apparatus brings the macula densa at the thick ascending limb/distal tubule junction into
contact with the vascular pole (area near afferent and efferent arterioles). It includes renin-producing granular
cells, predominantly in the afferent arteriole, and extraglomerular mesangial cells.
High NaCl delivery. Increased NaCl uptake through apical NKCC2 at the macula densa leads to ATP and
adenosine signaling. Adenosine acting on A₁ receptors increases afferent tone and helps lower GFR and distal
NaCl delivery.
Low NaCl delivery. Reduced macula densa NaCl uptake reduces the afferent constrictor signal and favors
afferent dilation. Prostaglandin E₂ promotes renin release. NaCl delivery also depends on upstream tubular
reabsorption, so it can change independently of GFR.

Sympathetic/RAAS and hormonal control
Strong renal sympathetic activation constricts renal arterioles through α₁ signaling, reducing RBF and potentially
GFR. Renal sympathetic β₁ signaling, reduced afferent perfusion pressure and low macula densa NaCl delivery
stimulate renin release.
Renin cleaves angiotensinogen to angiotensin I. ACE converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II. At modest levels,
angiotensin II preferentially increases efferent tone and supports glomerular pressure during reduced perfusion.
Higher levels can constrict both arterioles and lower RBF and GFR. Angiotensin II also stimulates proximal
sodium reabsorption, aldosterone secretion, thirst and vasopressin release. Aldosterone promotes distal sodium
retention, while vasopressin increases collecting duct water permeability.
Renal vasodilator prostaglandins buffer vasoconstriction, particularly during reduced effective circulating
volume. Natriuretic peptides promote natriuresis and can increase GFR. The classic ANP response combines
afferent dilation with increased efferent tone. Intrarenal dopamine can promote natriuresis and renal
vasodilation.

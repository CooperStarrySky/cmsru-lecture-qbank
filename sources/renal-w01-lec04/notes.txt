Renal handling and clearance
A substance’s urinary excretion depends on how much enters the tubule by filtration, how much returns to
blood by reabsorption and how much enters the lumen by secretion. Clearance expresses the resulting excretion
rate relative to its plasma concentration.

Excretion rate = filtered load − reabsorption rate + secretion rate
For a freely filtered solute, filtered load = GFR × plasma concentration. Excretion rate = urine concentration ×
urine flow rate.

Clearance measures
CS = (US × V) ÷ PS
US is urine concentration, PS is plasma concentration, and V is urine flow rate. When the concentration units
match and V is in mL/min, clearance is in mL/min.
Inulin measures GFR. Inulin is freely filtered and undergoes essentially no tubular reabsorption or secretion.
With U = 125 mg/mL, P = 1 mg/mL and V = 1 mL/min, GFR is 125 mL/min. An infusion and timed sampling make
the measurement impractical for routine use.
Creatinine approximates GFR. Creatinine is produced by muscle metabolism, freely filtered, and also secreted in
small amounts. Timed urine creatinine clearance therefore tends to overestimate GFR. If GFR falls abruptly,
creatinine excretion initially falls and plasma creatinine rises. With stable production and predominantly renal
elimination, excretion returns approximately to production at the new steady state. Serum creatinine-based
eGFR is calculated from a prediction equation. Muscle mass and rapidly changing kidney function affect
interpretation. In chronic kidney disease assessment, eGFR and urine albumin provide complementary
information.
PAH estimates effective renal plasma flow. At low plasma concentrations, filtration and proximal secretion
together remove about 90% of PAH in one pass. U = 5.85 mg/mL, P = 0.01 mg/mL and V = 1 mL/min yield CPAH =
585 mL/min, the effective RPF. Correcting for 0.90 extraction gives estimated total RPF of 650 mL/min.

How solutes and water cross the tubule
Transcellular movement crosses both the apical membrane facing the lumen and the basolateral membrane
facing the interstitium. Paracellular movement occurs through junctions between epithelial cells. After crossing
the epithelium, reabsorbed fluid and solutes can enter peritubular capillaries by bulk flow.

Proximal glucose reabsorption and transport maximum
Apical SGLT2 in the early proximal tubule reabsorbs most filtered glucose. Apical SGLT1 in the late proximal
tubule recovers the remainder. SGLT1 can reclaim more glucose when SGLT2 is inhibited and more glucose
reaches the late proximal tubule. Basolateral glucose exit uses facilitated diffusion through GLUT transporters,
classically GLUT2 early and GLUT1 later.
Filtered glucose load and Tm. At GFR 125 mL/min and plasma glucose 100 mg/dL (1 mg/mL), filtered load is 125
mg/min. Reabsorption rises with the filtered load until the transporters reach their aggregate capacity. Glucose
begins to appear in urine before the aggregate Tm is reached.
Clinical question on hidden slide 15. The best answer is direct inhibition of apical SGLT2 in the early proximal
tubule. Marked glucosuria despite lower fasting glucose is consistent with a lower renal threshold for glucose.
Basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase establishes the Na⁺ gradient that powers SGLT transport. Its inhibition could also
impair glucose uptake indirectly.

Proximal tubule reabsorption
A tubular fluid-to-plasma concentration ratio (TF/P) near 1 for sodium means water and sodium are removed in
roughly similar proportions. The amount of sodium left in the lumen still falls substantially. Glucose has TF/P
below 1 because it is reabsorbed faster than water. Chloride can transiently concentrate as bicarbonate and
water are preferentially reabsorbed. TF/P above 1 can reflect water removal outpacing solute removal,
secretion, or both.
